# Centaur Zendo — what the ladder taught us (report, 2026-09-05)

*One day of the optimisation ladder under the new format: 4 training rounds × 0.5 s, 7 classes per
game, 3 demos per team. 47 arena runs, 143 class-by-player finals, 115 class versions registered,
13 classes calibrated. Every number below comes from `ladder/state.json` and the player reports in
`sim/results/`; every quotation is a player's or judge's own words.*

## 1. The headline

Under this format a challenge class is **calibrated** when Opus-level centaur players crack it about
half the time and a Sonnet judge, playing a 12-year-old, scores it 4/5 or better for legibility. At
the start of the day zero classes met both tests. At the end, thirteen do:

## 2. What we learned

### 2.1 A class's difficulty is a property of which half of the picture the player has to produce

Kelmar carried the same relation through three versions — *a plant leans towards rain that reaches
the ground* — and scored 0 %, 5 % and 60 %. The rule never changed. What changed was the answer.
When the answer was the rain (the complicated half), the picture read as arbitrary blocks and the
plants read as decoration; when the answer became the small edit (a lean), the class was fair.
Basten told the same story across four versions (0 % for sixteen finals, then 100 % for the first
player who bought the demo), and tovel across seven. The house rule that came out of it:

> The clue is the finished object. The answer is the same picture with the smallest physical edit.
> One clause, said in one breath.

### 2.2 The checksum caption is the single most important design device

Both players in picture pool 4 said it unprompted, and every player since has repeated it: a
caption that **counts the edits** ("3 fall", "2 lean", "2 nibble", "2 turn") lets a team test a
hypothesis offline against the 70–100 clues they harvest for free in round one. A caption that is a
free parameter ("draw N fish") cannot be tested at all, so the class is demo-or-nothing, and nobody
spends a demo on a class whose answer shape is already obvious.

The checksum splits every class into two halves with different owners. The **selection** (*which*
things change) is findable offline by an AI from the count; players routinely prove it on 100+
clues before answering. The **rendering** (*what happens to them*: where a lorry ends up, that a
book's lid becomes a slash, that a cup lands on its side) is what the demo sells. When the rendering
was guessable from the picture (durnel v1: swap an arrow; molvic v1: swap two letters) both players
cracked the class blind at 100 %. When it was not, the class sat at ~12 % blind and 80–100 % with a
demo — the shape the format asks for. One player, having proved the selection rule for three
classes on 103/103, 123/123 and 116/116 clues and then scored zero on all three:

> "Knowing which N things are special is worth exactly zero without knowing what happens to them.
> Children look at the picture; I was looking at the arithmetic."

### 2.3 The demo cliff is the game

Across the last five picture pools the split is almost total: demoed classes 82–100 %, undemoed
classes 10–20 %, on every player. Players say they cannot tell from the clue which classes need the
demo, and that is the strategy the format asks for. The most interesting consequence was pointed out
by a player who asked us to keep it and "tell nobody": since the selection is free and only the
rendering needs the example, the optimal policy is to **demo the class you understand best**, not
the one that confuses you most.

### 2.4 Rule-family classes were beaten by a learning attack, not a reasoning one

The organiser's second paradigm — a seed-generated rule from a family, a minimal identifying set of
positive examples, an answer that is another instance — went through three revisions in a day.

* **Construct an answer** was beaten by predicate intersection: build 300 candidate predicates,
  keep the ones true of every example, emit an instance satisfying all of them. Nobody named a rule.
* **Choose from a lineup** (examples, then five candidates, exactly one fits) closed that door and
  opened another: answer a random candidate in round two, a fifth of them score, and thirty labelled
  examples per class arrive for free — "30× what a demo gives". From those labels the players learned
  each class's whole universe (which predicate uniquely explained each correct answer) and scored
  80–96 %. Every rule in those universes was already a cheap predicate in the players' feature banks
  (Jaccard 0.97–1.00 against a reconstruction of their code).
* **Revision 3** rebuilt every universe out of *relations between two elements or two positions*
  that a kid checks by eye and a feature bank does not contain: "the second bead matches the
  second-from-last", "every column of marks reaches the floor", "the two hands point at opposite
  numbers", "every heaped wagon is at one of the two ends". Simulated against the players' own engine
  at 0/30/60/120/240 labels the curve went flat at 35–55 %; the real players landed at 32–57 % on the
  next pool, and the one after. The ceiling for a player who invents the vocabulary is still 100 %.

Two measurement lessons came with it. The relational universe was worth ~55 points; aimed decoys
~8; the fifth candidate ~4; and "always the minimal example pair" — which we had reported as a
lever — was noise once the ablation was re-run at n = 1500. The recipe now forbids publishing any
lever delta under two standard errors.

### 2.5 The kid reading beat the statistics, every time it was tried

This is the finding the whole ladder was built to test, and it held in every pool:

* fennick: "the little towers fell over into the gaps" — the AI had spent twenty minutes on letter
  identities and alphabet positions.
* kelmar: the imagined kid said "clouds with rain under them"; the AI ignored it and parked on the
  foothold. The other player read the rain as "full-height trees" and cracked it — the physical
  reading was right even when the object was wrong.
* virel: "look — no brick is sitting right on top of another brick." The AI measured joints (the
  famous fact about walls) and lost an hour.
* basten: "the fish swam over and bit the top off the weed it could reach" — the first crack of the
  class after sixteen zero finals.
* mestrel: "that one has a domino with the same number twice on it, and none of the others do."
* wisbek: "a kid looks at the hands, not at h and m" — the AI scored 6 % on digits for two rounds.

And the failure mode is just as consistent: the AI over-trusts the first fitting hypothesis. One
demo fitted "slip the first N pairs of snare notes" by coincidence and cost a round; another player
decided the last example line of every lineup clue must be a *negative* example because no predicate
in its bank fitted both, and capped itself near chance on seven classes — when its own imagined kid
had said the right thing on the first demo.

### 2.6 What the players want, and what we changed

* Demos never show an identity (n = 0) example any more; a player lost one of three to a "0 bump"
  clue. Engine change, test, docs.
* The caption goes on the last line in every class (garrow had it first).
* The guide now says every example line in a lineup clue fits the rule.
* Requests we recorded but did not act on: property-scored answers ("the difference between a puzzle
  and a lottery" — virel accepts any valid course and was called the best class in the pool for it);
  a demo drawn to discriminate (one that refutes the commonest wrong reading); the throughput-versus-
  precision balance of the final ("skipping unsolved classes let me present 2010 items against
  1294"); and the fact that the players' engines can eliminate ~30 rules per class per round.

## 3. Case studies

### 3.1 fennick — the bookshelf (calibrated: 93 % with a demo over 12 players, 11 % without)

```
 _              _ __                          _              _ __
_E_            _J BR           _  _          _E_            _J BR           _  _
AEE  _         RJ BR  _      _ A  A          AEE  _         RJ BR  _      _ A  A
AEE  A_ _   __ RJ BR  B__    E A  A_    →    AEE  A_ _   _ /RJ BR  B__    E A  A_
AEE  AA J _ AR RJ BR_ BBB __ E A  AR         AEE  AA J _ A RRJ BR /BBB\  /E A  AR
AEE..AA.J.E.AR.RJ.BRB.BBB.JG.E.A..AR         AEE..AA.J.E.AR.RJ.BRB.BBB.JG.E.A..AR
====================================         ====================================
4 fall                                       4 fall
```

**Rule.** A book falls iff one side of it is a single empty slot, the other side is a touching
book, and the book across the gap is strictly taller; a fallen book keeps its foot, its body slides
one column into the gap, and its lid becomes `/` or `\`.

**Why it works.** The picture is the object; the caption names the verb and counts the edits; the
rule is one physical clause; every demo shows the positive case beside its three near-misses (equal
heights across a gap stand, a book with gaps on both sides stands, a two-slot gap is out of reach).
Nothing in the clue says *how* a fallen book is drawn, so the demo is genuinely needed, and once
seen the diff *is* the rule. It became the template for every picture class that followed.

**What it teaches.** The AI's first instinct on the demo was arithmetic (heights summing to four,
the four shortest); every player who cracked it did so by narrating the picture as a scene. The
human contribution here is not knowledge but *framing*: which question to ask of the picture.
The AI's contribution is verification at scale — one player confirmed the rule on 99/99 harvested
clues before answering, which no child would do.

### 3.2 kelmar — rain over a garden (calibrated: 76 % with a demo, 13 % without)

```
..(~~)....(~~~).....(~)....(~)...(~~)....        ..(~~)....(~~~).....(~)....(~)...(~~)....
..''''....'''''.....'''....'''...''''....        ..''''....'''''.....'''....'''...''''....
..''''....'''''.....'''....'''...''''....   →    ..''''....'''''.....'''....'''...''''....
..''''....'''''..................''''....        ..''''....'''''..................''''....
__*_*_Y_Y___*_Y____*____Y_Y_*___*___*_Y__        __*_*_\_Y___*_Y____*____Y_Y_*___/___*_Y__
2 lean                                           2 lean
```

**Rule.** A plant leans over for a drink iff the shower right next to it comes all the way down to
the ground. Showers that hang in the air are no use to anyone.

**Why it works.** The relation is the same one the class carried through two failed versions; what
changed is that the rain moved into the clue and the answer became the smallest edit. The near-misses
are planted in every picture (a flower standing in the rain, one directly under a hanging shower,
one beside a hanging shower, a tree two columns clear).

**What it teaches.** Neither player who cracked it saw rain — one saw trees, one saw mushrooms — and
it did not matter, because the relation they read (the column reaches the ground) is the rule. The
physical reading is robust to the wrong object. The one demo-holder who failed had a kid's reading
on the table ("clouds with rain under them") and overruled it with thresholds. The synergy is not
"the kid knows the answer" but "the kid's reading is a hypothesis the AI would not generate, and the
AI has the means to test it in seconds".

### 3.3 basten — the fish tank (calibrated: 100 % with a demo, 13 % without; sixteen prior finals at 0 %)

```
~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~        ~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~
.....................<><......        .....................<><......
.......................|......        .......................|......
.......................|......        .......................|......
......<><..|....><>....|...<><   →    ......<><.......><>....|...<><
...|..><>..|.....|.....|..<><.        ........><>|.....|.....|..<><.
...|...<><.|.....|.....|..<><.        ...|<><....|.....|.....|..<><.
##############################        ##############################
2 nibble                              2 nibble
```

**Rule.** Each fish looks along its row at the first weed in front of it; if the weed's top is in
the fish's row or one row above, the fish swims over and nibbles the top segment off.

**Why it works.** Three versions asked the player to draw N fish into an empty tank; N described
nothing in the picture ("n is provably not a level, a pole count, or any picture statistic"), so
nothing could be tested and nobody bought the demo. v4 put the fish in, made the caption count the
nibbles, and chose *vertical reach* as the one clause — deliberately orthogonal to horizontal
distance, after a first draft using blocking leaked ("the n fish closest to their weed" scored 89 %
for any demo-holder).

**What it teaches.** The judge gave it the highest object score in the collection: everyone names a
fish tank instantly. The question a kid asks first — "can it reach?" — is exactly the clause a diff
of two pictures does not reveal but a glance at the scene does. The AI's weakness is not
perception of glyphs but *asking the physical question*; its strength is that, once asked, it
verifies the answer on 44/44 clues and never misses again.

### 3.4 durnel — the road with low bridges (calibrated: 69 % with a demo, 10 % without; v1 was 100 % blind)

**Rule.** A lorry that cannot get under a bridge ahead of it pulls into the first lay-by it comes
to and turns round; it is erased where it stood and redrawn in the lay-by, nose the other way. A
lorry blocked before any lay-by has nowhere to turn and stays.

**Why it works.** v1 had no lay-bys; a blocked lorry swapped its nose. Both players cracked it blind:
the lorry-and-low-bridge fact is famous, the edit was guessable, and the checksum confirmed the rule
on 81 harvested clues before a single answer. v2 keeps the fact and the checksum but makes the
rendering carry the rule (where the lorry ends up depends on the first lay-by ahead) and plants a
lorry for which the famous fact is wrong. The blind reading went from 100 % to 0 %; the with-demo
rate has a real gradient (a player with the mechanism but the wrong tie-break for a contested lay-by
scored 24 %).

**What it teaches.** This is the cleanest demonstration that the two halves of the checksum design
are separable. An AI will find *which* lorries from the count. It cannot find *where they go*
without the example — and a kid who has seen one example says "it pulls in and turns round" in one
sentence.

### 3.5 tresk and ospren — the lineups after Revision 3 (calibrated: 38 % and 47 %; v2 was 79–87 %)

```
RRGRBGGBR                  .....   .....        1 #####  2 .####  3 .##.#  4 ###..  5 #####
RGGBGB                     ..##.   .....          #####    #...#    .###.    ##..#    #.#..
                           ..#..   ..#..          .#..#    #...#    .#.#.    .#..#    .....
GBRRRGG   GGGGGBR ✓        ..##.   ..##.          #...#    .#..#    .###.    ##..#    #.#..
BGRRGGG   GGBRRRG          ..##.   #.###          .....    .####    .##.#    ###..    #####  ✓
GGGBGGR
```

Left: beads — "the biggest clump is exactly five beads long". Right: pictures — "every column of
marks reaches the floor". Two examples, five candidates, exactly one fits.

**Why they work.** The universes are relations a kid points at with a finger and a feature bank does
not contain; decoys are aimed so that a wrong candidate carries an explanation at least as rare as
the truth's; two or three cheap, dense templates stay in at double weight so the class has a
learnable slope and is not arbitrary. The players' engines still run and still land at 35–55 %.

**What it teaches.** Both players said it in almost the same words: "my engine wins on arithmetic
families and loses on perceptual and phonetic ones"; "the true rules are all things you can see or
say in one short sentence; my engine kept proposing arithmetic no human would". The best moment,
one said, was realising that "the uniqueness constraint alone is a supervision signal" — which is
the AI's genuine edge: it turned the structure of the format into free labels. The human edge is the
vocabulary: the class is a lookup for whoever can *name* "the reds are all together".

## 4. What this says about AI capabilities

**Where the AI is strong, and stronger than any child.**

* *Enumeration and verification at scale.* Three to five hundred predicates per instance, tested
  against hundreds of harvested clues in under a second. Players verified rules on 99/99, 105/105,
  229/229 clues before answering.
* *Turning structure into supervision.* Random-candidate rounds as free labels; "exactly one
  candidate fits" as a self-check; the caption as a checksum. Every one of these was discovered by
  the players themselves, not told to them.
* *Speed and discipline.* 0.3 ms per answer, skip-what-you-cannot-solve, controlled experiments
  cycling hypotheses by index. The game's treadmill rewards this and the players are excellent at it.

**Where the AI is weak, and a 12-year-old is not.**

* *Reading a picture as a scene.* Every cracked picture class was cracked by narrating it ("the
  towers fell into the gaps", "the fish swam over"), and every failure was a spreadsheet of heights,
  distances and thresholds. The AI treats an ASCII picture as a data structure; the child treats it
  as a place where something is about to happen.
* *Asking the physical question.* "Can it reach?", "why did *those* fall?", "where would it go?".
  These are the clauses a diff does not reveal and a glance does. The AI does not generate them
  unprompted, and — the sharper finding — sometimes does not test them when its own imagined kid
  supplies them.
* *Rendering conventions.* Even with the selection proved exact, thirty-three plausible drawings
  scored zero. Once shown one example a child copies the convention without noticing there was one.
* *Over-trusting the first fitting hypothesis,* and reading a relation it cannot express as a
  contradiction in the data (the "negative example" player).
* *Vocabulary.* The lineup classes are a lookup for anyone who can name the relation; the AI's bank
  is large and the wrong shape.

**Where the synergy lies.** The centaur is not "the child knows and the AI computes". It is a
division of labour across the two halves of every class:

1. The child supplies the *framing* — the object, the verb, the physical question — as hypotheses
   the AI would not generate. The AI tests them against a hundred clues in a second and says which
   one is right. Every player who cracked a picture class did some version of this, whether or not a
   child was actually present.
2. The AI supplies the *bookkeeping* the child cannot do — the offline checksum, the label harvest,
   the per-class experiment design — and the child supplies the *rendering* after one example, which
   the AI cannot guess.
3. The demo decision is the shared strategic act: the child can often say which pictures are
   legible and which are opaque; the AI can say which classes it has already pinned offline and which
   it can probe. "Demo the class you understand best" is a policy neither would find alone.

The classes that work are the ones where both halves are needed. The classes that failed did so
because one side could do the whole job: too hard when the AI could test nothing (free-parameter
captions), too easy when it could test everything (guessable renderings, bank-shaped universes).

## 5. What to do next

* Two picture classes are calibrated on rate but below the kid bar (norvel 3.8, molvic 3.3): the
  rule is fine, the drawing is still bookkeeping. Tovel (kid 4.7) is 100 % with a demo and needs
  two more finals; felsim and garrow await their first demo-holders; tavrik v4 needs finals.
* Property-scored answers (virel's model) deserve a deliberate experiment: they leak the gradient a
  learner needs and the players called it the fairest class.
* Demo drawing: prefer an example that refutes the commonest wrong reading. The engine already
  refuses identity examples; the next step is per-class.
* Watch borsel and kaldrin: their cheap templates carry the crack and both trend above 55 %.
* Run a pool with a real child. Every "kid reading" above was an AI imagining one, and it still
  beat the AI's own statistics. The actual experiment has not been run.
